Photograph yourself all the way round

Four photographs and the program can work out your actual shape — not a face on a box, you, from every side. It does it by taking the outline of you in each shot and carving away everything that cannot be inside all four at once. That means the shots have to agree with each other, and these five things are what make them agree:
  1. Prop the phone up and do not touch it. A table, a shelf, a friend who can keep still. If the camera moves between shots, nothing below works.
  2. Lock the exposure. On a phone, press and hold on the wall until it says AE/AF LOCK, before the first shot. Left alone a phone re-meters between frames, and a wall that comes back darker in the next shot looks to the program exactly like something new standing in front of it. It is corrected for — but locking it is better than correcting it, and it takes one press.
  3. Hold your arms a little away from your sides. The one people skip, and the one that costs most. An arm touching your ribs cannot be told from your ribs by any outline, so the two fuse and the arms have to be guessed at.
  4. Stand well back, all of you in frame — head to feet, with a hand's width of room above and below — and a plain wall behind you that is not the colour of what you are wearing.
  5. Turn a quarter at a time, on the same spot. Face the camera, then your left shoulder to it, then your back, then your right.
Take the empty room first if you can — one shot with nobody in it. It costs five seconds and it is worth more than the other four put together, because then the program knows exactly what is room and what is you, including the shadow you throw on the floor.
Or take one video instead of any of it. Prop the phone, press record on the empty room, step in, turn one slow full circle, step out, stop. The first moment is the empty room and the rest are the turns — the program pulls out a still each time you have turned a little and drops everything else. It is the same photographs at thirty a second, and easier to take well. (A truly live camera needs a secure https page, which a home server is not — a recording is as close as a browser will allow, and you can retake it.)

1 · The empty room

One photograph with nobody in it, from the spot the phone will stay in. Optional — but it is worth more than all the others put together, because then the program knows exactly what is room and what is you, shadow included.

2 · Every turn, all at once

Pick them all in one go, or drop them on the window. As many as you like — twelve is plenty, twenty is better, and the program works out which way round each one was.

Or film it instead: prop the phone, start recording on the empty room, step in, turn one slow full circle — count ten — and step out. One video is the empty room and every turn in a single take: the program pulls the stills out itself, keeps one each time you have actually turned, and builds as they land. Another video adds to the pile — carry on turning from where the last one stopped rather than starting the circle again, because the pile is read as one long turn.
Tap a photograph to say that one is the front. The lit part of each is what the program found — if it has eaten your head, or taken in your shadow or half the wall, that is the shot to retake.
Everything else is unchanged — the same photographs, the same empty room. It carves only from the crown to the neck, so the whole grid goes on the head: about fifty cubes across a face instead of ten. The body keeps whatever it already had.

You, in cubes

20
What you are looking at is the visual hull: the tightest shape your outlines allow. It cannot see into a dimple and it cannot see the gap between an arm and a ribcage — which for a photograph would be a fatal limitation and for a man made of cubes is not a limitation at all. If it looks blocky at the corners, that is four outlines rather than eight — Take them again and fill in the diagonals.

The head is the part this does best, and there is a reason: a head is very nearly convex, and a visual hull is exact for convex things. A body is not — arms and legs stand off it and the hull can only be the tightest shape the outlines allow round all of them.
